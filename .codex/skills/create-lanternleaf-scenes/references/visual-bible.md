# Lanternleaf visual bible

## Audience and feeling

- Target ages 5–7.
- Make every scene safe, tender, curious, and gently magical.
- Favor one readable emotional beat over spectacle or dense world-building.

## Fern

- Small childlike fox with a full rounded body and large soft triangular ears.
- Rust-red/orange fur; cream muzzle, chest, ear interiors, and fluffy tail tip.
- Tiny black nose, rosy circular cheeks, and a small readable mouth.
- Round deep-brown eyes with one clear white catchlight and one tiny secondary highlight.
- Dusty-teal cross-body satchel with a simple flap and small gold fastener.
- Build fur from two or three broad transparent washes with gentle overlapping glaze boundaries. Keep large regions calm and mostly smooth. Do not simulate fur with scales, loops, leaf marks, hatching, repeated dabs, or digital airbrush shading.

## Pipkin

- Small floating moth friend with a rounded mint-green head and body.
- Two short antennae ending in warm-gold tips.
- Exactly four translucent golden leaf-shaped wings with restrained vein lines.
- Rosy circular cheeks and the same round highlighted eye language as Fern.
- Build the green body from one or two broad transparent washes with a soft organic tonal shift. Never fill it with circles, scales, leaf marks, or patch patterns.

## World

- The Lanternleaf Forest contains hollow-tree cottages, mushroom roofs, dusty-teal awnings, potted herbs, seed lanterns, stepping-stone paths, tiny bridges, ferns, and lantern-lilies.
- Suggest the world with a few chosen motifs. Do not render every available detail.
- Keep the environment lighter and looser than the characters. Let washes and incomplete forms dissolve into white.

## Medium and surface

- Use original children's-book ink and watercolor.
- Use a smooth bright neutral-white ground near `#FCFCFA`; it should appear almost textureless at viewing size.
- Preserve watercolor through transparent layers, visible overlapping glaze edges, soft wet-on-wet transitions, gentle edge pooling, a few irregular broad blooms, backruns, and loose dry-brush endings in the environment.
- Do not use paper tooth, embossed texture, canvas weave, fibers, speckles, beige tint, or yellow cast.
- Do not create repeated internal motifs in painted fills. At close crop, a colored region must read as uninterrupted color and gradual shading.
- Use natural watercolor marks at a large scale. Never tile a small brush symbol across a surface.
- Avoid digital-painting cues: airbrushed volume, bloom filters, radial glows, rim lights, perfectly smooth cinematic gradients, and glossy highlights.

## Line language

- Use thin warm-brown ink with mild natural pressure variation.
- Draw each exposed cheek, belly, head, limb, tail, body, antenna, and wing curve as one continuous confident stroke.
- Break a line only at a true occlusion, at a form junction, or at a rare plausible brush lift.
- Never use evenly spaced dashes, dots, beads, stitches, fragmented curves, or decorative gaps.

## Composition

- Use a 16:9 master with all essential characters, props, and actions inside the centered 4:3 region.
- Paint a compact vignette over roughly 55–60% of the canvas.
- Preserve roughly 40–45% true white negative space, especially around the upper and outer canvas.
- Avoid enclosing foliage, dark perimeters, full-bleed backgrounds, or visual clutter.

## Palette

- Fern: rust orange, warm cream, dusty teal.
- Pipkin: mint green, translucent warm gold.
- World: sage, moss, dusty teal, pale blue, warm ochre, small berry-pink accents.
- Night: one or two overlapping localized pale indigo washes plus restrained warm gold; keep surrounding white visible and use reserved paper for light.

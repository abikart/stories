# World-Building Visual Prose: The Bipedal Mythical Forest Glen

This guide is designed for creating scenes in a whimsical, watercolor-storybook world inhabited by bipedal animal denizens. It breaks down the style, composition, characters, and environment to ensure a consistent peaceful and storybook vibe, allowing for unique character and setting swaps while maintaining visual fidelity.

## How to Use This World Guide

This document defines the visual vocabulary and possibilities of the world; it is not a checklist of elements that must appear in every image. Build each scene around its specific story beat, location, character action, and emotional focus. Include environmental features when they are established by the scene or naturally enrich its composition. Recurring motifs should make the world feel related across scenes without making every setting look the same.

The written guide controls the world grammar. The images in this directory are
role-specific references rather than interchangeable style authorities:

- `worlds/lanternleaf/assets/characters/index.md` controls the current
  Lanternleaf cast names, species, pronouns, homes, and role status.
- `fern-and-pip.png` demonstrates an inhabited Mossgrove scene,
  character-to-world scale, and foreground layering. It does not control the
  identity of the current cast.
- `elephant-capybara.png` demonstrates bipedal collaboration, workshop density,
  and pale atmospheric distance.
- `hedge-frog.png` demonstrates bipedal tool use, grouped species features,
  handmade props, and an outdoor craft setting.
- `dino-reference.png` demonstrates only soft-cute species simplification. Its
  beige paper, full-bleed setting, and posture do not override this guide.

All land denizens are upright and bipedal. Explicitly flight-capable denizens
such as Zippy may hover while retaining the same simple, tool-capable character
language. Final story media is composed natively at 4:3 even when a reference
image uses another ratio.

Contextual does not mean minimal or empty. The world should feel abundant, inhabited, handcrafted, and worth exploring. Surround the central action with a varied selection of story-supporting details—plants, tools, vessels, lights, shelves, signs of work, small structures, or other locally meaningful objects—while avoiding the mechanical repetition of the same motif in every scene. Richness comes from the number and variety of recognizable ideas, not from scratchy rendering or microscopic surface marks.

## Overall Aesthetic & Medium

Scenes are rendered in a soft, handcrafted watercolor medium, presenting a high-key, airy vignette that floats centrally on a piece of pristine, clean, pure white paper. The unpainted surrounding space must be made solid, clean white with no aged or visible paper texture. The watercolor should be visible through confident broad strokes, smooth transparent tonal transitions, calm connected color inside forms, and occasional subtle pigment variation at natural boundaries—not through repeated blooms, mottled patches, a paper-grain overlay, or simulated surface noise.

Use a hybrid watercolor-and-ink approach. Define the main character silhouettes and essential props with controlled edges, then allow gentle continuous tonal transitions inside those shapes. Paint environments more loosely, letting broad strokes feather, overlap, and dissolve into the white field. Use distinct, fluid hand-inked contours to hold important forms together without adding surface texture. Do not use colored-pencil rendering, pencil shading, hatching, stippling, repeated short strokes, or dense surface marks.

## Composition & Perspective

The composition must remain open and airy. Floating vignettes bleed naturally into the clean white field. Deliberate, abstract, and unstructured white negative space is used noticeably, especially in distant areas, distant forest washes, and around the tips of foliage. This negative space prevents the composition from becoming heavy or enclosed. The horizon is defined by soft washes, not dense patterns. The perspective is cozy, inviting, and slightly close to ground level.

### Detail Hierarchy

Every scene must have a clear hierarchy rather than equal detail everywhere:

1. Faces, gestures, and the central story action are the clearest elements.
2. Character silhouettes, clothing shapes, and the primary prop receive moderate definition.
3. Plants, terrain, architecture, and distant scenery may contain many recognizable forms, but they are painted with broader marks, softer edges, and lower contrast than the focal action.

Favor one readable emotional beat, then reward a longer look with layered environmental storytelling. Leave the color within each individual form calm and uninterrupted, but allow many distinct forms to create a rich setting. A viewer should understand the characters and their action first, then discover the crafted objects, plant varieties, and small signs of life around them.

### Environmental Richness Without Texture Noise

Separate **semantic detail** from **surface detail**:

- Semantic detail is encouraged: distinct flower species, layered foliage, handmade objects, useful tools, decorated doors, hanging lights, potted herbs, small shelves, varied terrain, and other readable signs of an inhabited place.
- Surface noise is discouraged: hatching, stippling, pencil grain, individually rendered fibers, repeated moss flecks, countless fur strands, and tiny marks used only to simulate texture.

Build richness through overlapping shapes at several scales. Use a few large anchoring forms, a collection of medium-sized supporting forms, and small accents near the focal area. Establish foreground, middle ground, and pale background layers so the vignette has depth. Render each supporting element with economical watercolor shapes and a clear locally colored contour rather than filling its surface with marks.

## Generic Character Template (Bipedal Denizens)

All denizens (regardless of species, e.g., kits, cubs, turtles, frogs) share a round, soft-cute aesthetic: large rounded heads, compact torsos, short limbs, and simple readable silhouettes. Species should be communicated primarily through overall shape and a few defining features, not through dense fur, scale, feather, or skin rendering. The posture and species are swappable while the calm visual simplicity remains consistent.

### Posture

Characters are strictly bipedal, standing upright on two legs with human-like posture and tool-using capability. They stand and interact like upright denizens, not on all fours. They are capable of standing upright on irregular surfaces (e.g., stepping stones).

### Features

Very large, innocent, dark, and deeply reflective eyes, with delicate white highlights. Expressions are gentle, with small sweet smiles and soft blended rosy watercolor cheeks. Build fur, skin, feathers, quills, and other surfaces from one to three broad transparent washes with gradual tonal shifts. Keep interiors mostly smooth and quiet. Group complex features—such as hedgehog quills or tufts of fur—into a few clear silhouette shapes rather than drawing every strand or spine.

### Accessories

Small, handcrafted accessories, simple clothes (e.g., tunics, vests, aprons), and tools are common, but must feel integrated and artisanal, not digitally precise. Give important accessories a few readable construction details—such as a flap, clasp, pocket, tie, handle, bead, or seam—while keeping their painted surfaces smooth. Avoid visible fabric grain, excessive stitching, and ornamental micro-detail that does not reveal character or use.

### Paint and Contour Language

The watercolor fills are soft, but important forms have distinct hand-painted outlines. Every contour must be derived from the fill color of the exact shape it encloses: use a slightly darker, slightly more saturated version of that local wash. A green body receives a deeper green contour, a cream belly a soft warm-beige contour, a blue apron a deeper muted-blue contour, a pink petal a deeper rose contour, and a brown branch a deeper brown contour. Contour color should change naturally where the fill color changes. Never apply one uniform brown, black, or umber outline across the whole image.

Lines should be thin, smooth, fluid, and mostly continuous, with gentle hand-painted pressure variation. They must be clearly visible but remain close in value to their fills—only a modest step darker, not a heavy graphic border. Aim for the quiet contour strength of a simple watercolor character illustration: enough contrast to clarify the silhouette, but never so dark that linework dominates the washes.

Use a clear contour hierarchy:

- Give foreground characters the clearest and most complete outer contours so their silhouettes remain immediately readable, while keeping every segment locally color-matched.
- Use slightly thinner lines for facial features, limbs, clothing boundaries, and essential props.
- Give recognizable foreground and middle-ground environment elements—such as a branch, flower, door, pot, or bell—lighter but still distinct locally color-matched contours.
- Allow distant foliage and atmospheric washes to lose their outlines and dissolve into white.

Outline shapes and meaningful form boundaries, not surface texture. Do not trace every internal color change, leaf vein, hair, quill, moss patch, or watercolor bloom. Avoid fuzzy pencil edges, sketchy searching lines, doubled contours, hatching, and repeated broken marks. The result should feel clearly drawn with a fine watercolor brush or ink nib, then softly painted—not digitally cut out and not rendered with pencil.

## Environment

### Living Dwellings

Structures must be integrated directly into organic natural features like massive tree stumps, tree trunks, massive root systems, or organic mounds, blurring the line between building and nature.

**World vocabulary to combine contextually:** Rounded (e.g., blue-painted) doors with stone steps; whimsical scalloped awnings or roofs; leaf-like shingles; dome-capped dwellings (like large mushrooms or rounded stone); hanging organic lights (like acorn lanterns); small balconies; window boxes; worktables; hooks; signs; and multi-tiered plant shelves. A lived-in setting may use several compatible details together, but their selection should express that particular location rather than reproduce a fixed template.

**Constraint:** Avoid complex, multi-story, modern, or angular human-centric architecture.

### Woodland Gardens & Landscape

Woodland paths, when present, may be formed by irregular stepping stones through moss. Water features and crossings are contextual rather than default motifs: include a clear stream only when it belongs in the location or supports the story beat, and include a rustic, arched wooden bridge only when a crossing is established or compositionally useful. Do not add a stream or bridge merely to signal that the scene belongs to this world.

Flora should feel varied, layered, and abundant. Combine several distinct plant silhouettes, heights, and bloom shapes, with denser clusters near inhabited areas and selected individual plants made readable in the foreground. Paint foliage masses with broad overlapping washes, then clarify a purposeful selection of leaves, stems, and flowers with locally colored contours. Do not reduce the setting to a few generic green blobs, but do not describe every leaf or cover each plant with repeated texture marks. Flora frames and enriches the story action without hiding it.

**Recurring plant palette (combine several when they fit the scene):**

- Abundant ferns
- Groups of delicate, pendant-belled flowers (in soft, varying colors: pale white, soft pink)
- Spikes of dense small blooms
- Potted plants

Background forests are soft washes, not detailed patterns.

## Lighting & Color Palette

The overall lighting is soft, warm, gentle, and diffuse, creating soft, transparent shadows. The color palette is composed of muted, pastel tones: deep moss and forest greens, creamy whites, soft terracotta/rusts, and various soft blues, pale pinks, and yellows, all anchored by the clean, solid white field.

# Why the paths are fading

**Status:** Exploration v0.1

**Purpose:** Give the primary adventure a tangible, child-readable cause

**Working name:** The Long Rain

## Proposed cause

Lanternleaf has a naturally wet season when journeys become shorter and
neighbors spend more time close to home. This year the rain lasts much longer
than usual. It does not destroy the world, but it washes out small markers,
softens banks, scatters branches, and keeps residents from making ordinary
visits.

Every village becomes busy with understandable local work. Roofs need patching,
gardens need draining, shelves need restocking, and delayed tasks pile up.
People stop carrying bread, tools, letters, stories, and invitations between
villages. They mean to begin again when life settles down.

During the pause, the living paths rest too. Their leaves curl, familiar gaps
fill with plants, and routes that were once obvious become hard to distinguish.
When the weather clears, each village tries to repair the path at its own edge.
They trim branches, straighten signs, and wait for the route to return. The
forest keeps closing because a living path is not maintained from one end. It
becomes recognizable when travelers move between places and both ends welcome
the connection.

No one planned the disconnection. It grew from many small, reasonable choices.

## What a child can see

The story should reveal the cause through repeated physical evidence:

1. A pile of undelivered letters or useful parcels waits under cover.
2. A village has plenty of one thing but has run out of something its neighbor
   usually brings.
3. A carefully cleared route curls closed again because nobody arrives from the
   other end.
4. Fern's old map shows a path where the forest now looks ordinary.
5. Two travelers approaching one another make the route easier to recognize:
   leaves turn, stems lift, and a passable way emerges.

No character needs to explain that the forest represents friendship. The
audience sees that isolation and reconnection have physical consequences.

## Inciting incident

After the rain, Fern and Pip are given a small delayed errand to the neighboring
village. The familiar path begins clearly but ends at a screen of curled leaves
and crossed stems. Fern's map says to continue straight. Pip notices a few warm
leaf undersides angled along an unfamiliar side route.

They can safely turn home, but the errand matters and the signs suggest there is
still a way through. Following the irregular clues leads them to the neighboring
village by a route that is not on Fern's map. There they find more undelivered
items, a different missing path, and residents who assumed Fern's village was
still repairing the connection from its side.

Fern and Pip return home with the first useful evidence: the paths are not
simply damaged, and the old map is no longer enough. Their small delivery
becomes the reason to investigate farther.

## Why the young characters matter

Adults are capable and active. They repair homes, keep routes safe, share
supplies, and support the travelers. They have not solved the larger problem
because every village initially sees only its own entrance to the forest.

Fern, Pip, and their eventual companions become important because they cross
boundaries and compare what different villages know. Their strengths help them
notice a pattern that cannot be seen from one home:

- Fern compares the old map with new routes.
- Pip sees openings and landmarks from above.
- the Maker understands why ordinary repairs do not hold.
- the Listener realizes that each village is waiting for another to begin.

The children do not replace competent adults. They become the first people with
enough shared experience to understand the whole problem.

## Resulting adventure pressure

The rain is over, but another wet season will eventually return. More
immediately, isolated villages are beginning to lack ordinary things they rely
on one another to provide. The pressure grows gradually and remains
non-catastrophic: unfinished work, missed celebrations, unanswered letters,
lonely residents, and supplies that are useful in one village but needed in
another.

The central journey asks whether the friends can help the villages begin moving
toward one another before waiting becomes the new normal.

## Questions this proposal must survive

- Does the Long Rain feel native to Lanternleaf rather than like a convenient
  plot device?
- Is the inciting errand important enough for Fern and Pip to continue without
  making their adults seem reckless?
- Can the path response remain subtle while still being unmistakable to a young
  reader?
- Does the absence of a villain leave enough pressure and interpersonal
  conflict for the longer adventure?
- What specific exchange between the first two villages demonstrates the rule
  without explaining it aloud?
